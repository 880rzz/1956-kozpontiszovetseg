Why Do We Remain Hungarian?

A personal question that does not need one answer for everyone.

Home / Why Do We Remain Hungarian?

01—13 · ESSAY

“If I live somewhere else, why do I still feel connected to Hungary?”

Imagine a young person asking: “I no longer live in Hungary. I went to school somewhere else, I work somewhere else, and perhaps I do not speak Hungarian perfectly. Why do I still feel that Hungary matters to me?” There is no single answer that fits everyone. But the question itself makes sense.

Identity is not an exam or a passport

Identity is made up of the many connections through which we understand who we are: family, language, customs, memories, communities, and the way other people treat us. It is not a label that is handed to us once and never changes. Citizenship is a legal relationship with a state; national identity is a personal and social sense of belonging. They often overlap, but they are not the same. Adolescents and young adults often think about what their background and communities mean to them. Researchers also stress that identity develops through the interaction of a person and their surroundings [6].[6]

Small traces from childhood

Many things begin early: the language used at home, who tells us stories, which holidays we celebrate, and what music we hear. A family photograph, an old suitcase, a handwritten letter, or a dish learned from a grandparent does not create someone’s Hungarian identity by itself. It can offer a starting point for questions and stories. Smells, tastes, and sounds may bring back a setting or a feeling, but memory is not a video recording. It can change over time, and stories heard later can become part of how we imagine the past.

Family history is made together

A grandmother may tell the same story many times. Someone else may say very little, leaving only a date, a photograph, or half a sentence. Both can shape what a young person knows about their family, but silence by itself does not prove a secret or trauma. Research on family narratives describes how shared stories, everyday conversations, and family history can connect a person’s own life story with a wider past [4]. One historical case study by Vera Hajtó examines descendants of the Hungarian child-relief migration to Belgium in the 1920s, not the refugees of 1956. It shows how family letters, photographs, interviews, and later generations’ memory work can be compared [7]. A young person does not simply inherit a finished story: they ask questions, interpret it, sometimes find new sources, and connect it to their own life.[4][7]

A language can be a doorway, not a pass

Hungarian may feel especially close to people who first heard family stories or everyday words of affection in that language. But language ability is not a reliable test of how Hungarian someone is. One person may speak fluently without feeling strongly connected; another may speak only a little Hungarian but care deeply about their family background. A study of Hungarian diaspora schools compared institutions on three continents and explored language education and community life [5]. It offers a useful window, but it cannot show that every young person follows the same path. Learning the language can offer connection, not an exam.[5]

People move for many different reasons

People move to study or work, to join family, to seek new opportunities, or because they feel unsafe. These reasons should not be treated as if they were the same. Migration can involve loss, curiosity, relief, excitement, or a mix of feelings. Someone can find a home in a new country while keeping a connection to an earlier one.

1956: when a historic turning point reshaped family lives

After the suppression of the 1956 Revolution, many people left Hungary. A 1958 report by the UN High Commissioner for Refugees recorded about 200,000 Hungarian refugees who reached Austria and Yugoslavia; more than 170,000 were resettled in over thirty countries [1]. These are contemporary administrative figures, not a perfect person-by-person count of every border crossing. Totals can differ because reports count different periods, routes, or legal categories. The scale is clear, however: Hungarian communities took shape in many countries after the revolution.[1]

One historical event is not every family’s story

For some families, 1956 means escape, separation, starting again, or relatives left behind. In other families the revolution may feel less personal, or there may be no direct connection to it. It would therefore be wrong to say that 1956 explains every Hungarian person’s identity abroad. It can instead be an important historical setting through which some families talk about freedom, fear, difficult choices, and beginning again. Canada’s 1956 oral history collection preserves refugees’ own accounts alongside family photographs, letters, and documents; second- and third-generation Hungarian Canadians helped gather the material [2].[2][3]

Remembering is not the same as inheriting trauma

A family past can be painful. Flight, danger, separation, or a loss that was never discussed may affect how generations talk to one another. But it does not follow that every descendant “inherits trauma,” or that Hungarian identity always comes from pain. A systematic review of intergenerational trauma in refugee families found varied methods and findings and called for stronger research [9]. It did not specifically study Hungarian families displaced in 1956, so it cannot establish a single pattern for them. Personal memory, family stories, community memory, and trauma in the clinical sense can be connected, but they are not the same thing. A person can preserve a story without making its pain the centre of their own identity.[9]

Two homes do not have to pull you apart

A young person can be Hungarian and also part of Austrian, Canadian, German, American, or another society. These connections do not have to compete. They may study in one language and speak with a grandparent in another; their everyday friends may live in one country while family stories connect them to another. Multiple identities are not a mistake to fix. There is no need to choose between a new home and family origins, or to prove Hungarian identity through perfect language, particular customs, or a political opinion.

Why can this feel stronger at a distance?

Distance can make us notice what once felt ordinary: the sound of a language, a family celebration, or the questions people ask about where we come from. If someone feels like an outsider or is often asked “Where are you really from?”, they may think more about how they describe themselves. Discrimination can affect someone’s sense of safety and belonging, but it does not dictate which identity they must choose. Background can also take on new meaning in adulthood: someone may look up family places, learn more of the language, or simply understand an old story differently.

How does a memory become historical knowledge?

Historians and psychological researchers ask questions and compare different kinds of evidence. A recollection can tell us how one person experienced an event; by itself, it may not establish an exact date or prove that an entire community thought the same way. A researcher can compare an interview with letters, newspapers from the time, refugee records, photographs, and other testimony. For a photograph, they check its caption, the photographer’s and archive’s records, the visible setting, and whether other images or documents support the identification. Oral history archives—such as the life interviews of the 1956 Institute—are valuable sources, but access rules and the circumstances in which an interview was made matter too [8].[8]

The answer is not a single label

If you left Hungary when you were young, or grew up in another country, it still makes sense to feel connected to Hungary. That connection may live in your family’s story, a few words of a language, a photograph, a place, or your own questions. It may be stronger at some times and quieter at others. You do not have to feel exactly as your parents do, and you do not have to choose between a Hungarian connection and your new home. Being Hungarian need not be a test you pass. It can be a personal, changing relationship with the family and history you came from.

What is the evidence? Sources and limits

References

This essay brings together historical figures, oral history collections, peer-reviewed studies, and research reviews. They answer different questions. One refugee’s testimony shows an individual experience, not that of an entire generation; a study of one small community cannot describe every Hungarian in the diaspora.

  1. United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees. Report of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 1958, Chapter I: The Problem of Hungarian Refugees. Contemporary UN totals for the refugee movement after 1956.
  2. Simon Fraser University Library Digital Collections. 1956 Hungarian Memorial Oral History Project. Refugee life testimonies, family photographs, letters, and documents collected through second- and third-generation community work.
  3. András Lénárt. “Emigration from Hungary in 1956 and the Emigrants as Tourists to Hungary,” Hungarian Historical Review 1, no. 3–4 (2012), pp. 368–396. A historical study of later visits to Hungary by people who left in 1956.
  4. Robyn Fivush and Natalie Merrill. “An Ecological Systems Approach to Family Narratives,” Memory Studies 9, no. 3 (2016), pp. 305–314. A theoretical and developmental review of family stories and young people’s identity.
  5. Jenő Palotai, Viktor Wetzl and Ákos Jarjabka. “Identity Preservation and Hungarian Language Education in Diaspora Communities,” Hungarian Cultural Studies 12 (2019), pp. 108–124. A comparative study of three Hungarian schools on three continents; not a representative sample.
  6. Adriana J. Umaña-Taylor et al.. “Ethnic and Racial Identity During Adolescence and Into Young Adulthood: An Integrated Conceptualization,” Child Development 85, no. 1 (2014), pp. 21–39. A research synthesis on identity development in adolescence; it does not focus specifically on the Hungarian diaspora.
  7. Vera Hajtó. “The Past Must Be Given a Place”: Migration, Intergenerational Transfer, and Cultural Memory Practices in Belgian Families of Hungarian Descent, Hungarian Historical Review 12, no. 4 (2023), pp. 555–575. A memory case study of Hungarian families in Belgium concerning descendants of the 1920s child-relief migration, not the 1956 refugees.
  8. 1956 Institute Foundation, Digital Oral History Archive. Digital Oral History Archive catalogue. A catalogue of life-history interviews; access to individual records depends on interviewee permissions.
  9. Cindy C. Sangalang and Cindy Vang. “Intergenerational Trauma in Refugee Families: A Systematic Review,” Journal of Immigrant and Minority Health 19, no. 3 (2017), pp. 745–754. A systematic review of heterogeneous refugee-family studies; not a study of Hungarian families displaced in 1956.

This is an accessible overview for educational purposes. The cited research examines different communities and uses different methods; where direct evidence about the Hungarian diaspora is not available, that limitation is stated.
